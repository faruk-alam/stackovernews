Vinyl records outsold CDs for the sixth consecutive year and are now the most valuable physical music format for the first time since 1991, according to industry figures released this week. Revenue from vinyl rose 14 percent year on year, driven by a combination of major-label reissues, limited independent pressings and a younger audience that has never owned a CD.

## Supply can't match demand

The boom has exposed a bottleneck. Most of the world's pressing capacity dates from the 1970s and 1980s, and the machines are temperamental. Labels report lead times of six to eight months for new titles, forcing release schedules to be planned around plant availability rather than artistic timing.

"We have artists finishing an album in January and not being able to release it on vinyl until September," said one independent label manager. "Streaming goes out the same day, and the vinyl arrives when the moment has passed."

## New plants, new machines

The shortage has spurred investment. At least twelve new pressing plants have opened in the past two years, several using newly manufactured presses rather than refurbished vintage equipment. A handful are experimenting with injection-moulded records made from recycled plastics, which can be produced faster and with lower energy use — though audiophiles remain sceptical about sound quality.

## Who is buying

Survey data suggests the vinyl audience is split in two: collectors over 45 who are rebuying albums they once owned, and listeners under 30 for whom a record is as much a decorative object as a listening format. Roughly half of younger buyers say they do not own a turntable.

That has prompted artists to invest in elaborate packaging — gatefold sleeves, coloured vinyl, bound lyric books — that drives up the price and the margin.

## The environmental question

Vinyl is made from PVC, and its resurgence has drawn criticism from environmental groups. Several labels have committed to bio-based alternatives, and the largest plants now run on renewable electricity. Whether consumers will accept the slightly different look and feel of greener records remains to be seen.

Industry forecasters expect growth to continue, though at a slower rate, as new capacity comes online and the novelty fades. For now, the sound of a stylus dropping is also the sound of a cash register.
