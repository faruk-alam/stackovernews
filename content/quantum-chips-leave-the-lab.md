For years, quantum computing has been the technology that was always five years away. This week that timeline finally collapsed. **Helix Quantum** confirmed that its first commercially available, error-corrected processor has been installed at three customer sites, marking the first time a fault-tolerant quantum system has left a research facility.

The processor, codenamed *Aurora*, packs 1,024 physical qubits that are grouped into 48 logical qubits. Logical qubits are the crucial ingredient: by spreading one unit of quantum information across many physical qubits, the system can detect and correct the errors that have made earlier machines little more than expensive scientific instruments.

## Why error correction changes everything

Previous generations of quantum hardware suffered from decoherence rates that limited useful calculations to a few microseconds. Aurora's architecture extends that window to several seconds — long enough, the company says, to run chemistry simulations that would take a classical supercomputer weeks.

"We are no longer talking about demonstrations," said Dr. Priya Raman, Helix's chief scientist, during a briefing. "These machines are doing paid work for clients who care about the answer, not the physics."

### The first customers

The three launch customers come from very different sectors:

- A European pharmaceutical group simulating protein–ligand binding for a class of antiviral candidates.
- A global shipping company optimising container routing across 40 ports.
- A national weather service experimenting with ensemble forecasting.

Each installation is paired with a classical high-performance cluster that handles pre- and post-processing, with the quantum unit reserved for the narrow set of problems where it has a demonstrable edge.

## Skeptics remain

Not everyone is convinced the economics add up. Independent analysts point out that each Aurora system costs more than a mid-sized data centre, and that the software ecosystem is still immature. "Hardware is only half the story," said one researcher who asked not to be named because they advise a competing firm. "The algorithms that benefit from these machines are still being discovered."

Helix acknowledges the challenge and has released an open-source toolkit that lets developers test circuits on a cloud simulator before paying for time on real hardware.

## What comes next

The company plans a 4,096-qubit successor in 2028, and says a roadmap to one million physical qubits — the threshold many believe is required to break modern encryption — remains "a decade or more" away. For now, the message to enterprises is more modest: start experimenting, because the hardware is finally real.
