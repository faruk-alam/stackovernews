A capsule the size of a beach ball splashed down in the South Pacific early this morning carrying roughly 400 grams of material from one of the coldest places in the solar system: the floor of a permanently shadowed crater near the Moon's south pole.

The **Selene-3** mission, a joint effort between two national space agencies and a private launch provider, spent eleven days on the lunar surface. Its drill penetrated 1.2 metres into regolith that has remained below minus 230 degrees Celsius for an estimated two billion years.

## Why the ice matters

Orbital instruments have detected signs of water ice in these craters for more than a decade, but no mission had physically sampled it. Scientists want to know how much there is, how it is distributed and what else is mixed in.

The answers have practical implications. Water can be split into hydrogen and oxygen — rocket propellant — meaning a lunar base could refuel spacecraft without launching fuel from Earth at enormous cost.

"If the ice is as accessible as the early readings suggest, it changes the economics of everything beyond low Earth orbit," said mission scientist Dr. Tomas Alvarez.

## Early results

The capsule was flown to a curation facility where it will be opened in a nitrogen-filled glovebox to prevent contamination. Preliminary data from instruments on the lander indicate:

- Water ice concentrations of roughly **5 percent by mass** in the deepest layer.
- Traces of carbon dioxide and ammonia ice, hinting at a cometary origin.
- Fine-grained regolith with unusually low cohesion, which complicated drilling.

Full laboratory analysis will take months, and the team has promised to release results openly.

## The engineering challenge

Operating in permanent shadow meant the lander could not use solar panels. Instead it relied on a radioisotope heater and batteries, with a laser-communications relay on the crater rim bouncing data to an orbiter.

The drill itself jammed twice, forcing controllers to improvise a reverse-and-tap sequence that engineers compared to "freeing a stuck drawer."

## What comes next

A follow-up mission scheduled for 2028 will carry a small electrolysis demonstrator to test fuel production on site. Several commercial companies have already expressed interest in the data, and at least one plans to propose a dedicated ice-mining mission.
