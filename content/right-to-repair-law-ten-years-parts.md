Smartphone manufacturers selling into the bloc will be required to offer spare parts and repair documentation for at least ten years from the date a model is first sold, under rules finalised this week after three years of negotiation.

The regulation covers batteries, displays, cameras, charging ports and speakers, and caps the price of those parts at a "reasonable" margin above manufacturing cost. It also bans software locks that prevent third-party components from functioning — a practice known as parts pairing.

## A decade of support

Industry groups had lobbied for a five-year window, arguing that longer timelines would force them to warehouse components for devices almost nobody uses. Legislators rejected the argument, pointing to survey data showing that nearly a third of consumers keep a phone for more than four years, with many passing devices to family members afterwards.

"A phone that cannot be repaired is a phone that becomes waste," said the rapporteur who steered the bill. "We are simply asking manufacturers to stand behind their products for as long as people use them."

## What changes for consumers

From January, buyers should expect to see:

- A repairability score displayed at the point of sale, on a scale of A to E.
- Battery replacements available at authorised and independent repairers at a published price.
- Security updates for a minimum of seven years.

Independent repair shops, which have operated in a legal grey area for years, say the rules will finally give them access to genuine parts at predictable prices.

## Manufacturers adapt

Several large brands have already redesigned upcoming models with screw-fastened batteries and modular ports. One executive, speaking on background, admitted the changes were "less painful than expected" and may even reduce warranty costs.

Smaller brands are more worried. Maintaining a ten-year parts inventory across dozens of models is a logistical challenge that favours companies with scale. Some are expected to consolidate their product lines in response.

The rules apply to devices placed on the market after the effective date, so existing phones are unaffected. Regulators have signalled that laptops and tablets will follow in a second phase.
