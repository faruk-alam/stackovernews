Investors hoping for a steady path of lower interest rates were handed a reality check this week as two of the world's largest central banks signalled an extended pause, citing persistent price pressures in the services sector.

Headline inflation has fallen sharply from its peak, driven by lower energy and goods prices. But the cost of services — everything from haircuts to insurance to restaurant meals — continues to rise at nearly twice the pace policymakers consider consistent with their targets.

## Wages are the sticking point

Services inflation is closely tied to wage growth, and wages are still climbing at around 4.5 percent annually. Officials want to see that figure closer to 3 percent before they are confident inflation will settle.

"We have made real progress, but the last mile is proving to be the hardest," one governor said at a press conference. "We would rather hold for longer than cut prematurely and have to reverse course."

## Market reaction

Government bond yields climbed on the news, and equity markets gave back some of the gains built on expectations of cheaper money. Rate-sensitive sectors such as housebuilders and utilities were among the worst performers.

Currency markets were calmer, with the two currencies moving broadly in step as both banks adopted a similar stance.

## What it means for households

For borrowers, the pause means mortgage rates are likely to stay near current levels through the spring. Fixed-rate deals that had begun to fall are expected to stabilise.

Savers, meanwhile, will continue to enjoy returns on cash deposits that have not been seen in more than a decade. Analysts expect banks to trim the most generous savings rates only slowly.

## Looking ahead

Futures markets now price in the first cut of the new year in the second quarter, two meetings later than previously expected. Much will depend on the next few rounds of wage data, and on whether a cooling labour market translates into slower pay growth without tipping into outright job losses.
