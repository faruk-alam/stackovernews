Walk into any electronics store this season and you will notice something new: refurbished devices are no longer hidden at the back. They are on the front table, with the same lighting and the same warranty as the new models next to them.

The shift reflects a dramatic change in consumer behaviour. Market researchers estimate that refurbished devices made up 21 percent of smartphone sales in the last quarter, up from 9 percent three years ago. Laptops and tablets show a similar trend.

## Why now

Three forces are converging. Flagship phone prices have crossed psychological thresholds that make a two-year-old model at half the price newly attractive. Improvements in year-on-year hardware are increasingly marginal. And sustainability has moved from a niche concern to a mainstream purchasing factor, particularly among younger buyers.

"Our customers are not compromising," said Nadia Ferreira, who runs a chain of 40 refurbishment stores. "They are making a deliberate choice, and they expect the experience to match."

## The professionalisation of refurb

The industry has responded by investing in grading standards, automated diagnostics and multi-year warranties. Several manufacturers now run their own certified programmes, buying back devices and reselling them directly, capturing margin that previously went to third parties.

Retailers report that refurbished devices generate higher margins than new ones, because the supply chain is shorter and marketing costs are lower.

## Pressure on new sales

The flip side is softer demand for new devices. Analysts have trimmed forecasts for premium smartphone shipments, and some brands have responded by lengthening their release cycles.

Trade-in programmes have become a key battleground, with retailers competing to offer the highest value for old devices in order to secure the inventory that feeds their refurbishment pipelines.

## What to watch

Regulators are examining whether warranty terms for refurbished devices are sufficiently clear, and consumer groups want standardised grading language across retailers. If those issues are addressed, industry insiders believe refurbished could reach a third of the market by the end of the decade.
