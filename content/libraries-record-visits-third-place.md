Across hundreds of cities, public libraries are reporting their highest visitor numbers in decades — and not because more people are borrowing books. They are coming to work, to learn, to charge their phones, and increasingly simply to be around other people.

The phenomenon has revived a term coined by sociologists in the 1980s: the "third place," a space that is neither home nor work where community happens informally. With cafés growing more expensive and shopping centres emptying, libraries have quietly filled the gap.

## More than books

The most-visited branches have expanded far beyond lending. A survey of 500 library systems found that in the past three years:

- 72 percent added free co-working space with reliable Wi-Fi.
- 44 percent now lend tools, musical instruments or sewing machines.
- 31 percent run a community kitchen or food-sharing programme.
- 28 percent provide free access to a mental-health counsellor at least one day a week.

"A library has always been the one public building where you can walk in and nobody asks why you are there or expects you to buy anything," said Imani Clarke, director of a city library network that saw visits rise 40 percent. "That turns out to be rare, and valuable."

## The funding paradox

The surge in demand has not been matched by budgets. Many systems are serving more people with fewer staff than a decade ago, and some have introduced volunteer-run opening hours to extend access.

Advocates argue that the data make a case for investment. Early studies suggest that neighbourhoods with active libraries report lower loneliness scores and higher civic participation, though researchers caution that the direction of causation is unclear.

## Design for gathering

New library buildings reflect the shift. Architects describe a move away from silent reading rooms towards layered spaces: a loud, social ground floor; quieter study zones above; and small rooms that can be booked for anything from job interviews to language exchanges.

Several cities have begun to co-locate libraries with health clinics and childcare, treating them as the anchor of a civic campus.

## Looking forward

Whether the trend survives a return to pre-pandemic habits remains to be seen. But librarians are confident. "People found us when they needed us," Clarke said. "They are not going to forget we are here."
