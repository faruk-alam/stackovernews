Two years after receiving a single injection into the inner ear, eleven children born with a rare form of genetic deafness can hear well enough to follow conversation in a quiet room, according to results published this week in a peer-reviewed journal.

The children were born with mutations in the *OTOF* gene, which produces a protein called otoferlin that inner-ear hair cells need to signal the auditory nerve. Without it, the ear detects sound but cannot pass the message to the brain.

## How the therapy works

The treatment uses a harmless virus to deliver a working copy of the gene directly to the cochlea. Because otoferlin is too large to fit in a single viral vector, researchers split the gene in two and designed the halves to recombine inside the cell — a technique that had never before been used in humans.

"We were cautiously optimistic after the six-month data," said lead investigator Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou. "What we did not expect was how durable the effect would be, and how quickly the children's language caught up."

## The results

Of the eleven participants, aged one to seven at the time of treatment:

- Ten gained hearing thresholds in the mild-to-moderate range, compared with profound deafness before.
- Nine are now able to understand speech without lip reading.
- The youngest participants have language development on par with hearing peers.
- No serious adverse events were recorded.

One child did not respond, which researchers attribute to an immune reaction that neutralised the vector before it reached the target cells.

## Limits and caution

*OTOF*-related deafness accounts for only around two to eight percent of congenital hearing loss, so the therapy will not help the majority of deaf children. And the trial was small and uncontrolled.

Deaf community advocates have also urged researchers to frame the results carefully. "Deafness is not a disease to be cured for everyone," said one advocate. "Families should have the choice, and that choice should be informed by Deaf adults as well as doctors."

## The road to approval

The team has begun a larger trial with 60 participants across five countries. Regulators have granted the therapy expedited review status, and a decision on approval could come within three years.

Researchers are already adapting the approach to other single-gene causes of hearing loss, several of which are more common.
