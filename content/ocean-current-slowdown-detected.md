A network of autonomous sensors strung across the North Atlantic has recorded the clearest evidence yet that one of the planet's most important ocean circulation systems is weakening, and doing so faster than most climate models predicted.

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, or AMOC, carries warm surface water north from the tropics and returns cold water south at depth. It is a major reason western Europe enjoys mild winters relative to its latitude.

## What the floats found

The monitoring array, deployed over the past five years, measures temperature, salinity and current speed at depths of up to 4,000 metres. Analysis of the full dataset shows the circulation has slowed by approximately **8 percent** since monitoring began — a rate that, if sustained, would exceed the upper bound of most projections.

"We built this network because we knew the models disagreed," said oceanographer Dr. Ingrid Solheim. "What we are seeing is closer to the pessimistic end of the range."

## Why it is slowing

The leading explanation is freshwater. Melting from the Greenland ice sheet and increased rainfall are diluting the salty surface water in the far north, making it less dense and less likely to sink — the process that drives the whole system.

The data also show a warming of the deep return flow, consistent with heat being absorbed by the ocean interior.

## What it could mean

A significantly weaker AMOC would likely bring:

- Cooler, stormier winters in northwest Europe, even as the planet overall warms.
- Shifts in rainfall patterns across West Africa and the Amazon.
- Faster sea-level rise along the eastern seaboard of North America.

Scientists stress that a complete shutdown — the scenario popularised by disaster films — remains unlikely this century. But the new data have prompted calls to revise the probability upwards.

## Next steps

The research consortium has secured funding to extend the array southward and to keep it operating for at least another decade. Modelling groups are already incorporating the new measurements, and an updated assessment is expected within eighteen months.

"The ocean is telling us something," Solheim said. "Our job is to listen carefully and not overreact — or underreact."
