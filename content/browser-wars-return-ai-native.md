The web browser, long considered a solved problem dominated by two engines, is suddenly contested territory again. Three well-funded startups launched or updated browsers this quarter that put a language model at the centre of the browsing experience, and incumbents are scrambling to respond.

## From tabs to tasks

The pitch is simple. Instead of opening a dozen tabs to compare flight prices, you describe what you want and the browser does the clicking. Instead of reading a 4,000-word terms-of-service page, you get a one-paragraph summary with the red flags highlighted.

"People don't love browsers, they love what browsers let them do," said Jun Park, founder of **Compass**, which reported two million weekly active users after its public beta. "We're removing the browser from the equation."

Compass's agent mode can:

1. Book restaurant reservations across multiple booking platforms.
2. Track price changes on a watched product and alert you.
3. Draft replies to web-based email using the context of the page.

## The privacy question

Each of these features requires the browser to read, and often transmit, page content. Compass says it processes most requests on-device using a compact model and only sends anonymised snippets to the cloud for complex tasks. Competitors **Lumen** and **Trail** have made similar claims, though none has published a full third-party audit.

Digital rights groups are unconvinced. "A browser that reads everything you read is the most sensitive piece of software on your machine," said a spokesperson for the Open Web Foundation. "Promises are not a substitute for verifiable architecture."

## Incumbents respond

The two dominant browser vendors have each announced AI features of their own, but both have been cautious about agentic automation, citing security concerns around prompt injection — the risk that a malicious web page could instruct the AI to take actions on the user's behalf.

That caution may be the startups' opening. Analysts estimate that AI-native browsers hold under one percent of the market today, but note that market share in browsers has historically shifted quickly once a product gains a reputation for being genuinely faster.

Whether that reputation sticks will depend less on demos and more on how these products handle the first major security incident. Few in the industry believe that moment is far off.
