The results are in from the largest four-day working week experiment ever run, and they are hard to argue with. Of 180 companies that took part in the 12-month trial, 166 have decided to keep the shortened schedule permanently.

The participating firms — ranging from a 12-person design studio to a logistics company with 2,400 staff — cut working hours to 32 per week with no reduction in pay. Researchers from three universities tracked revenue, staff retention, sick days and self-reported wellbeing throughout.

## The numbers

Across the cohort:

- Revenue rose an average of **4.1 percent** compared with the previous year, broadly in line with the wider economy.
- Voluntary resignations fell by **38 percent**.
- Sick days dropped by **27 percent**.
- Employees reported a 0.9-point improvement on a 10-point burnout scale.

"The productivity question is settled," said Professor Amara Okafor, who led the research team. "The interesting question now is how organisations change the way they work to make it possible."

## How companies made it work

The most common changes were mundane. Firms cut meeting lengths by default, introduced "focus blocks" when internal messaging was discouraged, and gave teams autonomy to choose which day to take off so that customer-facing roles remained covered.

A software company in the trial replaced its weekly all-hands with a written update. A manufacturing firm moved to staggered shifts so machines ran five days while individuals worked four.

## The holdouts

The fourteen companies that reverted cited client expectations and coordination problems with partners who remained on five-day schedules. Several said they intend to try again once suppliers adopt similar patterns.

Critics note that the trial was self-selecting: companies that volunteered were likely already well managed. The research team concedes the point and is planning a randomised follow-up with a larger pool.

For now, the signal to employers is clear enough that recruitment firms report job listings advertising a four-day week attract roughly twice as many applicants as comparable five-day roles.
